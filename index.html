3. Reading: The Long History of Distance Shopping

Paragraph 1: Ancient Trade Tablets. Long before internet servers and automated emails existed, merchants carried out distance orders using clay tablets. In 1750 BC, a Babylonian customer named Nanni sent a formal written complaint on a cuneiform tablet to an ore merchant named Ea-nasir. Nanni had sent messengers through hazardous desert territory with silver, only to receive copper ingots of inferior quality. His written protest, demanding fair treatment and threatening to reject future deliveries, is recognized by historians as the oldest surviving consumer complaint in human history.

Paragraph 2: The Mail-Order Revolution. Distance purchasing changed dramatically in the late 19th century with the expansion of transcontinental railways and postal systems. In 1872, Aaron Montgomery Ward in Chicago published the first single-sheet mail-order catalogue, offering rural farmers goods that had previously been available only in big cities. By standardizing item descriptions and introducing the radical promise of "satisfaction guaranteed or your money back," catalog shopping established the modern standards of returns, refunds, and remote customer service.

Paragraph 3: Upcycling and Modern Ecological Commerce. Today, distance commerce has expanded beyond mass-manufactured goods toward niche sustainable trade. Online platforms such as Tecoart specialize in upcycled craftwork, turning discarded computer hard drives, circuit boards, and clockwork components into functional art. While the technological medium has shifted from Babylonian clay to railway catalogues and finally to web portals, the fundamental commercial contract remains unchanged: the seller must provide accurate descriptions, and the buyer expects reliable delivery and respectful assistance when problems arise.

4. Grammar: Modals of Politeness & Requests (Can, Could, Would)

Grammar in Commercial Dialogues: Politeness Strategies

Direct commands ("Give me my money back", "Cancel this") are inappropriate in professional customer service.[cite: 21] English uses modal auxiliaries and conditional structures to soften requests:

1. Polite Inquiries and Offers:
• Can / Could I put you on hold for a moment? (Asking permission)[cite: 20]
• Would you like me to cancel the order, or would you prefer a replacement?[cite: 20]

2. Formal Written Requests:
• Could you please provide the tracking number?[cite: 21]
• I would be grateful if you could refund the amount to my credit card.[cite: 21]

3. Indirect Questions:
• Direct: When will it arrive? → Indirect: Could you tell me when it will arrive? (Word order: Subject + Verb).
